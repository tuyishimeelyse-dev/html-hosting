Smallholder farmers in Rwanda cultivate highly fragmented landholdings averaging 0.4 ha (with 77% managing under 0.5 ha), making multi-crop intensification indispensable for household food security and income generation. Across two agro-ecologically distinct research stations—Karongi (humid, well-distributed rainfall) and Kayonza (semi-arid with frequent dry spells)—this multi-cycle investigation evaluated spatial arrangements, component plant population densities (PPD), early planting, and the transition from Conventional Tillage to Conservation Agriculture (CT-CA vs. CT-CT) in Cassava + Maize/Beans intercropping systems.

Core Analytical Breakthrough — Why Full 12-Month Accounting Reverses Early Rankings: Preliminary assessments based solely on Season A maize and Season B bean harvests favored Low Cassava arrangements (F and G, 8,333 cassava/ha) because reduced cassava density minimized early competition on cereals. However, incorporating final 12-month cassava tuberous root yields, Area-Time Equivalent Ratio (ATER), and cumulative 12-month net profit overturns that conclusion and establishes a mandatory agro-ecological split: in humid Karongi, prioritizing high cassava density with moderate maize (Treatment E) unlocks massive tuber bulking (33.5–54.5 t/ha) and peak system wealth ($12,127–$14,175/ha), whereas in semi-arid Kayonza, below-ground moisture competition severely penalizes intercropped cassava, making sole cassava (B) or wide-alley spacing (C/D) essential.

1. Introduction, Research Gaps, Objectives & Hypotheses

1.1 Smallholder Context and Rationale

Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) occupies approximately 17% of Rwanda’s cultivated land—trailing only bush beans (22%) and bananas (18%)—and serves as a foundational food-security and cash crop for resource-constrained smallholders farming sloping, acidic, or degraded soils. Because 77% of Rwandan farming households cultivate less than 0.5 ha (national mean ~0.4 ha), farmers routinely intercrop cassava with maize and bush beans to harvest multiple food groups from the same plot within a single 12-month agricultural cycle.

While roughly 40% of One Acre Fund (OAF) farmers practice informal mixed intercropping, adoption of structured row intercropping remains low (~5%, despite generating ~$8/adopter in prior MEL assessments) due to four unresolved agronomic knowledge gaps:

1 Conservation Agriculture (CA) Compatibility

Absence of empirical data on transitioning from conventional tillage in Season A to no-tillage with retained maize stover mulch in Season B (CT-CA) within 12-month cassava systems across Rwandan AEZs.

2 Spatial Geometry & Density Trade-Offs

Uncertainty in row spacing and component plant population densities (PPD) needed to balance Season A/B cereal-legume yields against 12-month cassava root bulking.

3 Multi-Crop Nutrient Partitioning

Interspecific N–P–K competition and lateral fertilizer spillover between vigorous cereals and long-duration cassava under split-application regimes.

4 Canopy Shading & Microclimate Dynamics

Light interception by maize in Season A and expanding cassava canopies in Season B affecting understory bush bean pod set across wet vs. dry environments.

1.2 Trial Objectives & Formal Hypotheses

Objective 1

Spatial & Density Optimization

Quantify how spatial row arrangements and component densities affect maize, bean, and cassava yields, land-use efficiency (ATER), and cumulative 12-month net profit.

Objective 2

CT-CA vs. CT-CT Transition

Determine whether transitioning to zero-tillage in Season B (CT-CA, Treatment F) incurs any yield, cost, or profit penalty relative to continuous tillage (CT-CT, Treatment G).

Objective 3

Residue-Driven Weed Suppression

Evaluate whether Season A maize stover biomass retained on the soil surface suppresses Season B weed pressure and hand-weeding labor costs under minimum tillage.

Testable Hypotheses (H1 – H4)

  • H1 All cassava + maize/beans intercrop arrangements will increase system land-use efficiency (ATER > 1.0) relative to sole cropping across both stations.
  • H2 Full-density intercropping (Treatment D) will outperform the standard Akilimo arrangement (Treatment C) due to wider inter-row spacing (150 cm vs. 100 cm) between cassava rows.
  • H3 Low-cassava intercropping (Treatment F, cassava reduced by 17% to 8,333 plants/ha) will generate lower total 12-month system output than full-density cassava arrangements.
  • H4 The CT-CA transition system (F) will perform equivalently to continuous CT-CT (G) because Season B bush beans tolerate zero-tillage under retained maize stover mulch.

2. Experimental Design & Statistical Methodology

Karongi Station (Western Province)

Humid High-Rainfall AEZ
  • Moisture Regime: High total precipitation with reliable distribution across both Season A and Season B (only 3 months exceeding 15 dry days between Oct 2024 and Jun 2025).
  • Biophysical Consequence: Abundant soil moisture supports simultaneous root-zone uptake by maize/beans and vigorous canopy & tuberous root bulking in cassava.
  • Cultivars: Cassava NAROCAS 1; Maize APIS 301; Bush Beans RWR 2245.

Kayonza Station (Eastern Province)

Semi-Arid Dryland AEZ
  • Moisture Regime: Semi-arid savanna transition zone characterized by lower total precipitation and frequent dry spells (>15 dry days in nearly every month except April).
  • Biophysical Consequence: Intense below-ground water competition during Months 1–4 restricts cassava tuber initiation when paired with fast-rooting cereals.
  • Cultivars: Cassava NAROCAS 1; Maize WH 403; Bush Beans RWR 2245.

High-Density Maize + Low Cassava (G & F) Maximizes Cereal Output

Maintaining full maize density (53,333 plants/ha) while widening cassava spacing to 150 × 80 cm (8,333 plants/ha) eliminated interspecific yield penalties on maize. In Karongi Cycle 1, G (9.18 t/ha) and F (8.92 t/ha) matched or exceeded sole maize A (8.87 t/ha), aided by lateral nutrient spillover from NPK 17-17-17 applied to cassava at 6 WAP. In Karongi Cycle 2, G (6.20 t/ha, group d) and F (5.83 t/ha, group cd) significantly outperformed sole maize A (3.48 t/ha, group a), reflecting cumulative soil fertility benefits in multi-crop plots.

Stover Biomass Divergence Governs Downstream Mulch Supply

In Karongi, Season A maize stover biomass remained consistently high across both cycles (6.8–10.2 t/ha in Cycle 1; 5.8–9.9 t/ha in Cycle 2, peaking at 9.9 t/ha in F). Conversely, in semi-arid Kayonza, drought stress in Cycle 2 caused stover biomass to collapse from 4.0–6.5 t/ha in Cycle 1 down to just 1.0–3.6 t/ha (E ~1.0 t/ha, C ~1.3 t/ha, F ~2.8 t/ha), depriving Season B zero-till beans of protective ground cover.

Section 3.4 • Spatio-Temporal Efficiency

System Area-Time Equivalent Ratio (ATER)

Dataset: T1_04_ATER
Figure 3.4: Trial 1 Median System ATER across Arrangements (Dashed Line = 1.0 Monocrop Parity)

Interpretation: Because ATER weights each component’s Land Equivalent Ratio by its field occupancy duration over 12 months, ATER > 1.0 confirms true temporal and spatial land-use superiority over sole cropping. In Karongi, 100% of intercrop treatments exceeded 1.0 in both cycles: in Cycle 1, Treatment E reached a median ATER of 1.86 (mean ~1.95, group b), significantly outperforming G (median 1.34, group a)—indicating that a monocrop farmer would require ~90% more land to match 1 ha of Treatment E. In Cycle 2, all Karongi intercrops maintained high ATERs (medians 1.29–1.66, group a). Conversely, in Kayonza Cycle 1, severe cassava yield depression pulled median ATER below 1.0 across all treatments (0.69–0.94, group a), recovering to near-parity in Cycle 2 (0.96–1.33, group a) only because sole-crop controls also declined under drought.

Tillage Transition Contrasts (CT-CA [F] minus CT-CT [G]) & Residue Weed Suppression

Dataset: T1_06 & T1_09
Figure 3.6: Season A Maize Stover Biomass (t/ha) vs. Season B Weeding Labor Cost (USD/ha) under CT-CA vs. CT-CT
Mechanistic Synthesis (The ≥5 t/ha Residue Threshold): Across all 24 paired contrasts isolating CT-CA (F) vs. CT-CT (G) at identical plant densities, 22 contrasts showed zero statistically significant difference (p > 0.16). 8. Conclusions & Agro-Ecologically Segmented Recommendations

8.1 Key Conclusions

1. Cassava Density & Cost Efficiency Optimizing early cereal density at the expense of cassava (F/G) or over-packing multi-species seed (D: $1,901–$2,541/ha cost) sacrifices net returns. In humid corridors, Treatment E balances moderate costs ($1,075–$1,459/ha) with peak cassava bulking (33.5–54.5 t/ha) and profit ($12,127–$14,175/ha).
2. Weeding Labor is Year-2 Bottleneck Hand-weeding labor surged to 45%–84% of total production costs in Cycle 2 ($647–$1,490/ha). Economic scaling requires rapid canopy closure and surface mulch retention to suppress weeds.
3. Inputs & Timing Are Non-Negotiable Omitting 8 t/ha compost and mineral fertilizer cuts cassava profits by >52% even when planted on time, while slipping into late Oct/Nov planting without inputs wipes out 78%–93% of annual Maize–Beans profit.
4. Conservation Ag Needs ≥5 t/ha Mulch Zero-tillage in Season B (CT-CA) saves labor (-$38/ha in Karongi) only when Season A yields ≥5–6 t/ha maize stover; below 3–4 t/ha, hand-uprooting adds +$204/ha in labor costs.
Zone 1: Humid / High-Rainfall AEZ e.g., Karongi & Western Corridor

Primary Scaling Package: Treatment E under CT-CA

  • ✓ Spatial Arrangement: Plant cassava at 120 × 80 cm (10,417 pl/ha) with 1 center row of maize at 60 cm (33,333 pl/ha) in Season A, followed by 2 rows of bush beans (250,000 pl/ha) planted zero-till into retained maize stover in Season B.
  • ✓ Input & Timing Protocol: Pair with 8 t/ha basal compost + split NPK/DAP/Urea and enforce strict early/mid-October planting to capture peak 12M profit ($12,127–$14,175/ha).
  • ✓ Secondary Option: Where households prioritize higher maize grain self-sufficiency, deploy 100% Density (D) (150 × 70 cm cassava + 2 maize rows), yielding 5.61–7.94 t/ha maize and $8,325–$10,468/ha net profit.
Zone 2: Semi-Arid / Dryland AEZ e.g., Kayonza & Eastern Province

Commercial Sole Cassava (B) or Wide-Alley Intercrops (C / D)

  • ✓ Commercial Cassava Growers: Prioritize Sole Cassava (Treatment B, 100 × 100 cm) on dedicated plots ($3,793–$5,764/ha net profit; lowest weeding labor at $432.5/ha in Trial 2) rather than full-plot intercropping.
  • ✓ Land-Constrained & Late-Planted Smallholders: Deploy Akilimo (C) ($1,494–$3,028/ha) or Wide-Alley D (150 cm rows, ATER = 1.03, $2,372/ha in Trial 2). Where planting slips into late Oct/Nov without full inputs, cassava intercropping (F) provides critical downside protection ($1,140–$5,861/ha) vs. annual Maize–Beans ($114–$228/ha).
  • ✓ Adaptive Residue-Triggered Tillage Rule: Transition to no-till Season B planting (CT-CA) only if Season A maize stover exceeds ~5 t/ha (>70% soil cover); in drought years with <3–4 t/ha stover, permit shallow hand-hoe weeding (CT-CT) to avoid the +$204/ha labor penalty.
